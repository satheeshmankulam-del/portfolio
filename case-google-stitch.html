Google Stitch —
A Small Menu, A Big Difference

How a single overlooked context menu in Google's Stitch creates cascading friction — and how one small design change could fix the entire experience.

Product
Google Stitch
Type
Friction Audit
Focus
Context menu UX
Published
Medium · Design Bootcamp
01 — Problem

The menu you can't find
when you need it

A design tool that makes designers work to find basic design actions is a product that misunderstands its own user.

Google Stitch is a design tool — users are actively creating and editing. At the moment of highest intent (just selected an element, ready to act), the most-needed actions are hidden behind an overflow menu buried in a corner of the toolbar. The problem isn't that the actions don't exist — they do. The problem is that their location creates unnecessary context-switching at the exact moment when a user should be in flow.

02 — Research

How a small menu
breaks big flows

🔍
Actions happen in context
When a user selects an element, their mental model immediately expects contextual actions — not a journey to a toolbar. Every pixel of distance between object and available actions is friction.
💡
Cognitive load of search
Having to remember where an action lives pulls the user out of creative flow and into navigation mode. For a design tool, this is particularly costly.
📐
Established design tool patterns
Figma, Canva, and Adobe XD all surface contextual toolbars on selection. The mental model is established. Deviating without good reason creates unnecessary relearning.
⚡
Small fix, large impact
A floating contextual toolbar that appears on selection — with the 4-5 most common actions — eliminates the majority of navigation friction without structural redesign.
03 — Design Process

Surface the actions
to the action

01
Identify the core friction point
Mapped the user journey to find exactly where — and why — the experience breaks down. Focused on the highest-impact moment rather than trying to fix everything at once.
02
Research existing patterns & alternatives
Studied how comparable products handle the same scenario. Identified design patterns that reduce the friction without adding cognitive complexity.
03
Design the intervention
Created wireframes and interaction models targeting the core problem. Prioritised the simplest change that produces the biggest experiential improvement.
04
Publish & validate thinking
Documented the full analysis and proposed solution in a Medium article, inviting critique and discussion from the broader UX community.
04 — Outcomes

The principle of
contextual proximity

1
Toolbar interaction
to access any action
0
Navigation to toolbar
for common actions
∞
Design flows
that stay unbroken

Actions should live where the intent lives. When a user selects something, they're ready to act — the design should meet them there.
